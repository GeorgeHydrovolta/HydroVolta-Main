Comparison

Electrodialysis vs reverse osmosis for high recovery desalination.

Both desalinate. They differ in how much of the feed you keep, how much brine you have to get rid of, and whether the minerals survive the process. On a difficult inland source those three things usually decide the project.

Every figure below comes from one completed pilot — cooling tower blowdown at a chemical industry facility in Germany, Feb–May 2023, a 90-day head-to-head against RO and standard EDR on the same feed water at TDS 4,500 ppm and silica 85 mg/l. A different feed water will give different numbers.

How they differ

One rejects everything. The other moves selected ions.

Reverse osmosis

Pressure forces water through a semi-permeable membrane and leaves the dissolved solids behind. Rejection is by size and charge, not identity, so everything goes — including the calcium and magnesium. It needs 25–35 bar to do it.

Electrodialysis reversal

An electric potential pulls selected ions through ion-exchange membranes and out of the product stream. Transport is driven by charge, so the process can be configured to remove some ions and retain others, at 0.5–2.5 bar.

What the numbers mean

Where the difference actually lands.

Recovery decides the brine

Recovery and brine volume are the same number seen from either end. Moving from the RO range to the SonixED™ range is what turns disposal from a project-ending cost into a manageable one, which is the whole of brine minimisation.

Pressure decides the energy

RO needs high pressure because it pushes water through a barrier. Electrodialysis moves ions instead, so it runs an order of magnitude lower and the energy figures follow from that rather than from a better pump.

Scaling decides whether it keeps running

Anti-scalant dosing is the usual answer to hard, silica-laden water. Ultrasound-assisted scaling control removes that consumable, which is why the CIP frequency differs rather than the membrane being better.

Honest limits

When reverse osmosis is the better answer.

Choose RO

Full seawater at around 35,000 ppm TDS, where SWRO is typically preferred. Sources where full demineralisation is genuinely the objective. Applications needing removal of organics or pathogens, which an ion-selective process does not address.

Choose selective electrodialysis

Brackish sources in the 1,500–20,000 ppm TDS range. High silica or high hardness that fouls membranes. Inland sites with no discharge route. Cases where the minerals should stay in the water.
